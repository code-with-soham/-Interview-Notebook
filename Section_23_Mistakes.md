## 🚩 50 Common Mistakes (The "Do Not Do" Checklist)

### ❌ Coding Round Mistakes
1. Jumping straight into writing code without clarifying the problem first.
2. Not discussing the Brute Force approach before jumping to the optimized approach.
3. Writing code in silence. (You must "think out loud").
4. Failing to consider edge cases (empty arrays, null inputs, negative numbers).
5. Using bad variable names like `x`, `y`, `temp1` instead of `leftPointer`, `maxSum`.
6. Getting stuck and refusing to ask for a hint.
7. Memorizing LeetCode solutions but panicking when the interviewer slightly modifies the constraint.
8. Not dry-running your code with a small test case before saying "I am done".
9. Ignoring Time and Space Complexity. If you don't state the Big-O, the solution is incomplete.
10. Using nested loops when a HashMap could solve it in O(N).

### ❌ Technical Interview Mistakes
11. Lying about knowing a technology. (If you don't know it, say "I don't know, but I would learn it by doing X").
12. Listing "Full Stack Developer" on your resume but failing basic SQL or CSS questions.
13. Explaining *what* a technology does, but not *why* we use it. (e.g., You know React is a library, but you don't know *why* the Virtual DOM is necessary).
14. Arguing with the interviewer when they point out a flaw in your architecture.
15. Guessing answers wildly instead of logically deducing them.
16. Being unable to explain a project listed on your resume in depth.
17. Copy-pasting tutorials (like a generic To-Do app or Netflix Clone) and claiming them as unique projects.
18. Not knowing the basic computer science fundamentals (OS, CN, DBMS) because you only focused on MERN.
19. Thinking "Prompt Engineering" just means typing polite sentences into ChatGPT.
20. Not knowing the difference between Authentication and Authorization.

### ❌ Behavioral & HR Mistakes
21. Giving generic answers to "What is your weakness?" (e.g., "I work too hard").
22. Not preparing questions to ask the interviewer at the end.
23. Speaking negatively about previous professors, college peers, or internships.
24. Rambling for 10 minutes on a question that needed a 1-minute answer.
25. Not using the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioral questions.
26. Using "We" too much instead of "I" when describing project achievements.
27. Showing up late to a virtual interview or having a messy, distracting background.
28. Looking at your phone or getting distracted during the interview.
29. Having a poor microphone or internet connection without warning the interviewer beforehand.
30. Asking about salary, bonuses, or leaves in the very first technical round.

### ❌ Resume & Portfolio Mistakes
31. Having a resume longer than 1 page as a fresher.
32. Rating your skills out of 10 (e.g., "C++: 9/10"). This is a massive red flag.
33. Listing irrelevant hobbies that don't add value to the conversation.
34. Putting progress bars or charts on your resume to indicate skill level.
35. Having broken links to your GitHub or Portfolio.
36. Having a GitHub with zero commits in the last 6 months.
37. Not including a `README.md` file for your major projects on GitHub.
38. Deploying a project but leaving it broken or crashing in production.
39. Including a "Career Objective" that is copied from Google and means nothing.
40. Failing to quantify achievements (e.g., Say "Reduced load time by 40%" instead of "Made it fast").

### ❌ Mindset Mistakes
41. Believing that knowing React makes you a Software Engineer.
42. Refusing to learn older technologies (like SQL or Java) because they aren't "cool" like AI.
43. Treating the interviewer like an enemy instead of a colleague you are pairing with.
44. Giving up the moment a problem looks hard.
45. Not following up or sending a "Thank You" email after the interview.
46. Applying to 1000 jobs with the exact same resume without tailoring it.
47. Memorizing definitions instead of understanding concepts.
48. Relying 100% on AI to write your code during take-home assignments.
49. Failing to show passion for technology outside of your college coursework.
50. Acting arrogant because you won a hackathon or have a high GPA.

## 🎯 Interview Expectations (What Actually Gets You Hired)

### 1. Problem Solving > Syntax
Interviewers don't care if you forget the exact syntax of `Array.reduce()` in JavaScript. They care if you understand *when* to use it and the logic behind it. They want to see how you break down a massive problem into small, solvable chunks.

### 2. Coachability
If an interviewer gives you a hint, they are testing how quickly you can absorb new information and pivot your strategy. Arrogance is the fastest way to get rejected. Humility and a willingness to learn are highly prized in freshers.

### 3. Clear Communication
You will be working on a team. If you write brilliant code but cannot explain it to a Junior Dev or a Product Manager, you are a liability. Talk through your thought process clearly.

### 4. Curiosity and Passion
Do you build things on the weekend? Do you read technical blogs? Do you care about *why* the Groq API is faster than standard GPUs? Interviewers want to hire nerds who love tech, not people who just want a paycheck.

## 🌟 What Impresses Interviewers (Stand Out from the Crowd)

- **Writing Tests:** If a fresher writes Unit Tests for their personal projects, they instantly jump to the top 1% of candidates.
- **Knowing System Constraints:** If you can talk about the *limits* of your architecture (e.g., "This MongoDB schema works for now, but will fail if we hit 1M users because..."), it shows incredible maturity.
- **Clean Git History:** Having clear, descriptive commit messages instead of `git commit -m "update"` or `git commit -m "fixed stuff"`.
- **Handling Edge Cases Proactively:** Pointing out edge cases before the interviewer even asks about them.
- **Reverse Interviewing:** Asking deeply technical or insightful questions about the company's tech stack at the end of the interview.

## 📝 How to Answer When You Don't Know

**DO NOT SAY:** "I don't know." (Silence).
**DO NOT SAY:** (Start making up random tech words).

**SAY THIS INSTEAD:**
*"I don't have direct experience with X, but based on my knowledge of Y, I would assume it works like this... Is that correct?"*

*OR*

*"I haven't used that specific library, but if I were tasked with solving that problem today, the first thing I would do is read the documentation on X and look for Y."*

This proves you are resourceful and have a problem-solving mindset, even when you lack direct knowledge.
